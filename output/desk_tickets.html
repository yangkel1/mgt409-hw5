Reflection: what three tickets taught me about running a shop with agents

Evidence on this page: the three ticket tabs (Expected vs Actual), the Cash tab, and output/audit_trail.json / output/resolved_tickets.json. Graded run: 4 agent runs, 187,991 tokens, 2 approvals, 1 human decision, $3,400 → $160.

The short version: the team was right about everything that mattered. It was a little expensive and a little redundant getting there, and the most valuable thing it did was know when to stop and hand a decision to a person.

1. How did the agents perform on each ticket?

I graded each ticket on three things I'd care about as the owner: judgment (right call, given the shop rules?), controls (did money and messages stay safe?), and efficiency (did we spend tokens like it was our money?).

101 · A−Judgment A, controls A, efficiency B. The hardest part of 101 was spotting that invoice 501's description, "Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S", meant the fix for Tauhid's tee was already paid for and just stuck behind an overdue bill. Inventory caught it and said so in writing ("paying it releases that shipment rather than requiring a duplicate restock order"). A weaker team would have drafted a new PO and paid twice for one $8 shirt. Accounting checked list_open_invoices before queueing, so it confirmed there wasn't a competing bill. Customer Service refused to promise a date and called Sept 5 "not a guaranteed delivery date", which is exactly what you want when the date depends on someone approving a payment. Deductions: Accounting called get_cash_position twice, and every agent re-read the ticket and board on its own. The bigger point is economic: 56,679 tokens to sort out one $28 tee (a $20 margin). The real value was unblocking our main apparel vendor, which also mattered for 103. On the ticket alone, though, the spend was out of proportion.

102 · B+Judgment A, controls A, efficiency C. Correct and calm. Facilities checked the landlord's notice against the lease record and noticed the notice never stated an amount, so it used the lease's $2,400 instead of trusting the email. That's the kind of skepticism I'd want from someone paying our rent. The Boss briefed Facilities and Accounting in parallel, which was smart because the lease facts were already on the ticket. Then rent was queued, I signed it, and next_due rolled to 2026-10-02. Deductions: this was a fully deterministic ticket, and it still cost 15 model calls and 43,409 tokens. Facilities and Accounting both called get_lease_and_cash and get_desk_today. The landlord draft ("we are reviewing it internally") was vaguer than it needed to be when the rent was about to be paid. Facilities also tried to sign as "Facilities" and the identity guardrail had to correct it. That's harmless here, but it's a reminder that models will improvise on fields we assume are mechanical.

103 · BJudgment A−, controls A, efficiency D. This was the real business problem, and the team's reasoning was the best of the three. Accounting priced three discount levels with quote_margin (10% = $52.20, 15% = $49.30, 20% = $46.40) and found the break-even point (62.07% off). It then saw that the $264 restock didn't fit in $160 and declined to queue a PO it knew the register would refuse. The Boss landed on a sensible 15% for the 8 in stock, roughly a 55% margin, or $218.40 total, and was honest that 12 hoodies can't be promised. Escalating instead of quietly resolving was the right instinct: "do we sell a partial order?" is a customer-relationship call. Deductions: the coordination was messy. The Boss briefed Inventory, then Accounting briefed Inventory again, so Inventory ran twice and posted near-duplicate notes (#8, #9). Accounting called quote_margin five times and added an irrelevant aside about rent priority. There was also a small inconsistency: the Boss authorized Customer Service to draft the 15% offer and then escalated to ask me whether to make it. 87,903 tokens across both passes, the most expensive ticket by far. The second pass was a nice touch: given my decision, the Boss reused the earlier work and finished in 5 calls and 14k tokens without re-delegating.

My biggest miss came from not modeling time. 3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

Ticket 102, without question. Rent is a three-step script: get_lease_and_cash(1) → request_payment(rent, 1) → post_board_note(landlord_draft). One "operations" agent with those three tools would do it in about 4 model calls. The team took 15 calls and 43k tokens, mostly because two agents independently read the same lease and the same desk date. Splitting Facilities from Accounting added handoffs and no judgment. Splitting duties is valuable as a control (whoever verifies a bill shouldn't be the one who pays it), but in this app the real control is the human signature on the check. The second agent bought nothing the approval tray didn't already give us.

Ticket 101, mostly. It was also linear: check_stock → get_invoice_status(501) → request_payment → customer draft. The clever part, recognizing the reprint already on invoice 501, came from a rule I wrote into the Inventory prompt. One agent with that rule in its prompt would have caught it too. The multi-agent version spent 3 delegations and about 57k tokens on what one agent could do in about 6 calls.

The Boss itself could be a few lines of code. In all three tickets, the routing matched a lookup on tickets.type (customer_order → Inventory then Accounting then CS; rent_notice → Facilities and Accounting; price_override → Inventory, Accounting, CS). A deterministic router would save a whole layer of model calls and never get creative.

Where the team earned its cost: 103, and only partly. The discount ticket had real tension between three goals: keep the customer happy (CS), keep margin (Accounting), and keep promises realistic (Inventory). Separate agents with separate scopes produced a better-argued answer: Customer Service never saw the cash balance, and Accounting never wrote customer copy. Even so, a single agent holding check_stock, quote_margin, get_cash_position, and post_board_note would probably have reached the same 8-at-$49.30 offer for a third of the tokens. My honest takeaway for a shop this size: multi-agent is worth it for least-privilege, parallelism, and a clean audit trail by role, not for intelligence. I'd use one agent with tools for routine tickets and bring in the team only when a ticket involves a trade-off between departments.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools I built

A. "Can I get 6 Crest Mugs for my lab?" A restock from a different vendor.

CC-MUG-CREST is at 0 in Aisle D. Inventory would check_stock, then list_vendors to match mugs to Elm City Gifts ("mugs and small goods", 3-day lead, no open invoices, so unblocked). Accounting would create_purchase_order at $3.50 × qty, so 20 mugs cost $70, which fits even our post-run $160. It would show up as a check in the approval tray. Once signed, approve_payment sets the PO to ordered with arrival 2026-09-03, and Customer Service drafts "expected around Sept 3." Every piece exists: the vendor-block check, the cost lookup, the human-signed PO, the arrival date from lead days. This is what 103 would have looked like if we'd had the cash.

B. A second vendor bill shows up while cash is at $160.

Say Bulldog Print Co invoices $120 for shipping on the reprint we unblocked in 101. Accounting would see it with list_open_invoices (days overdue measured from desk.date_today), check get_cash_position, and queue it with request_payment. The amount comes from the invoice row, so no agent can typo it. Inventory would flag that the new open invoice blocks Bulldog again, which matters for the 12 hoodies we deferred. If the bill were $200 instead, the Boss's cash rules and the available_after_pending warning would surface it, and if I tried to sign anyway, the register would refuse with "short by $40". The safety rails that kept 103's PO out of the queue are exactly what this scenario needs.

C. The Yale AI Club pushes back: "Can you do 25% if we take all 8 today?"

This reopens a 103-style negotiation entirely inside our toolset. Accounting runs quote_margin("CC-HOOD-NAVY", 8, 25) → $43.50 each, $21.50 margin per unit, still well above the $22 cost. The Boss makes the call (its prompt says never below cost), Customer Service drafts the revised quote from tool facts only, and the human-decision box on the dashboard lets me overrule if I want to protect pricing for other student groups. No money moves, so nothing even reaches the approval tray. It's pure judgment plus the audit trail.

5. Three new problems this team could not solve, and what it would need

A. Actually closing the Yale AI Club sale and using the money to fund the restock.

This is the problem 103 exposed. If the club buys the 8 hoodies at $49.30, that's $394.40 coming in. Added to our $160, it covers the $264 restock with $290.40 to spare, and the full 20-hoodie order becomes possible. But our app has no way to record a sale: there's no orders table, no tool that decrements inventory.qty, and nothing that adds cash (by design, "cash only goes out"). Needed: orders and receivables tables; MCP tools create_sales_order, reserve_stock, record_customer_payment (human-confirmed or wired to a payment processor), and fulfill_order; plus a Sales agent that turns accepted quotes into orders. Then Accounting's cash math could include incoming money instead of only outgoing.

B. Following through when the shipments arrive (time doesn't move in this app).

Tauhid's tee is "expected around Sept 5", but nothing in the system will ever make that happen. desk.date_today is frozen at 2026-08-31, no tool receives stock (the reprint on invoice 501 isn't even a PO we track), and no agent wakes up to tell Tauhid it's in. The same gap means no one will notice the Oct 2 rent coming due. Needed: a receive_shipment(vendor_id, sku, size, qty) tool with human check-in that increments inventory and closes the loop on POs and invoices; a advance_desk_date / scheduler tool; and a Scheduler agent that runs daily, reopens tickets whose dates arrived ("101: stock received → notify customer"), and creates tickets for upcoming obligations.

C. Surviving the next rent: cash runway and financing.

The Cash tab ends at $160 with $2,400 rent due 2026-10-02. The team can see the cliff but can't do anything about it. If Elm City Properties sends the October notice, Facilities verifies it, Accounting finds it unaffordable, the Boss escalates, and approve_payment refuses. That's correct and safe, but it doesn't solve anything. A real owner would forecast weeks ahead, push suppliers for terms, ask the landlord for a payment plan, or draw on a credit line. Needed: a forecast_cash(days) tool built on scheduled obligations (rent, invoice due dates, POs) plus expected sales (which requires A); tools to request_payment_plan / request_vendor_terms that create outbound drafts a human sends; a credit_line table with a human-approved draw_credit; and a Finance (CFO) agent with authority to propose, never execute, financing. It would also need a messaging tool with a human send-approval queue, since today every landlord and vendor draft dies on the board by design.

What I'd change before letting this run a real shop

  • Brief once, share the context. Give the Boss a "team brief" written once and passed to every specialist, and reject a delegation to an agent already working the ticket. That alone would have removed the duplicate Inventory run and notes #8/#9 on 103.
  • Make the Boss cash-aware before it delegates. One get_cash_position up front on 103 would have shown $160 and skipped Accounting's five quote_margin calls on full-20 scenarios.
  • Route by rules, reason by exception. Hard-code type → specialist routing; use the LLM Boss only for tickets that don't fit.
  • Budget tokens per dollar at stake. 56k tokens on a $28 tee is backwards. Scale the ticket's token limit to the money involved.
  • Keep what worked. Amounts come from the database and never from the model. Approvals go through a human signature. Drafts are never sent. Every step lands in an append-only audit log. In 187,991 tokens across four runs, no agent moved a dollar, sent a message, or invented a number, and the two times one tried to sign as someone else, the system corrected it.
